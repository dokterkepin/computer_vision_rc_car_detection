\section{Introduction and objective}
\label{sec:intro}

The goal is to build a global-vision server for an RC-car playing field.  A
camera is mounted on a selfie stick or tripod at a side angle so that the
whole field is visible.  Image measurements are converted to the real
two-dimensional field coordinate system using camera calibration and a
planar homography.  The required output for each detected car is

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{timestamp} & relative time in microseconds\\
\textbf{car ID} & identifier of the detected car\\
\textbf{position} & $(x,y)$ in millimeters, or $(-1000,-1000)$ if absent\\
\textbf{orientation} & $\theta$ in degrees\\
\textbf{velocity} & $(dx,dy)$ in millimeters per second\\
\textbf{angular velocity} & degrees per second\\
\textbf{image position} & $(u,v)$ pixel coordinates of the car\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Notation.}  Field coordinates $(x,y)$ are in millimeters.  The
$x$-axis points from AprilTag ID~0 to ID~1, the $y$-axis from ID~0 to ID~3
(Section~\ref{sec:tags}), and the orientation $\theta$ is measured
counter-clockwise from the $+x$ axis, seen from above.  The velocity is
$(v_x,v_y)=(dx,dy)$ and the angular velocity is $\omega$.

\textbf{Reading the screenshots.}  The program's on-screen overlay, console
output and CSV files label the two field coordinates in the order
\emph{(y, x)}.  Only the \emph{labels} are in that order: the first printed
number is the $x$ of this report and the second is the $y$, and likewise the
printed velocity pair is $(v_x,v_y)$.  For example, the handwritten note
``724, 145'' (marked $y$, $x$) in Fig.~\ref{fig:cases} means
$(x,y)=(724,145)$\,mm.  Every equation and table in this report uses $(x,y)$.

For each frame the server \textbf{(1)} captures and undistorts the image;
\textbf{(2)} detects the car with SAM3 and obtains its box and mask;
\textbf{(3)} maps the box center through $H$ to obtain $(x,y)$ in
millimeters; \textbf{(4)} estimates $\theta$ from the mask; and
\textbf{(5)} uses consecutive timestamped measurements to calculate linear
and angular velocity.  It publishes
\texttt{timestamp:ID,x,y,theta,vx,vy,omega,u,v}, with every missing field set
to $-1000$ (and $(u,v)=(-1,-1)$) when no car is found.

\section{Car detection and tracking}
\label{sec:detect}

Ultralytics SAM3 detects the RC car with the text prompt \texttt{toy car}.
SAM3 is an open-vocabulary segmenter: the prompt is converted to a vector by a
text encoder and compared with image regions, so the car is found without any
training on our data.  For each image SAM3 returns, per instance, a
confidence score $s\in[0,1]$, a bounding box
$(u_{\min},v_{\min},u_{\max},v_{\max})$ and a binary segmentation mask.  Only
the instance with the highest score is kept, and it is accepted when
$s\ge 0.3$.  The model runs in half precision with an internal input size of
448~px for the whole project (the camera frame itself is $1920\times1080$).
This gives about 13--15 frames per second on an NVIDIA RTX~4060~Ti in the
overlays of this report.

\textbf{Crop tracking.}  The car occupies only a few hundred pixels of the
frame, so after the first detection SAM3 is run only on a square window
around the expected car position.  The window is resized to the model input,
so the car covers more pixels and is detected more reliably.  (The run time
is set by the model input size and not by the window size, so the benefit is
accuracy, not speed.)  With $\mathbf c_t$ the box center at frame $t$ and
$d_t=\max(u_{\max}-u_{\min},\,v_{\max}-v_{\min})$ the box size, the center of
the next window is the prediction
\[
\hat{\mathbf c}_{t+1}=\mathbf c_t+\mathbf q_t,\qquad
\mathbf q_t=0.6\,\mathbf q_{t-1}+0.4\,(\mathbf c_t-\mathbf c_{t-1}),
\]
where $\mathbf q_t$ is a smoothed estimate of the pixel displacement per
frame, and its side length is
\[
w_{t+1}=\max\bigl\{\,320\ \text{px},\ 4\,d_t\,(1+\tfrac12 m)\bigr\},
\]
with $m$ the number of consecutive frames without a detection, so the window
widens while the car is lost.  After more than five consecutive misses the
system returns to a full-frame search.

\section{Camera calibration}

The lens causes distortion, so pixel coordinates are corrected before
mapping.  A point $(X,Y,Z)$ in camera coordinates has normalized coordinates
$a=X/Z$, $b=Y/Z$ and $r^2=a^2+b^2$.  With the radial coefficients
$k_1,k_2,k_3$ and tangential coefficients $p_1,p_2$ of the OpenCV model, the
distorted coordinates and the resulting pixel position are
\begin{align*}
a_d &= a\,(1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6)+2p_1ab+p_2(r^2+2a^2),\\
b_d &= b\,(1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6)+p_1(r^2+2b^2)+2p_2ab,\\
u &= f_x a_d + c_x,\qquad v = f_y b_d + c_y,
\end{align*}
where $K=\begin{bmatrix}f_x&0&c_x\\0&f_y&c_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$ is the camera
matrix and $\mathbf d=(k_1,k_2,p_1,p_2,k_3)$.  A printed Charuco board
(\texttt{DICT\_6X6\_250}, $7\times5$ squares of 25\,mm, markers of 18\,mm) is
observed in at least 15 different positions and orientations.  OpenCV detects
the Charuco corners, whose positions on the board are known, and
\texttt{cv2.calibrateCamera()} finds $K$, $\mathbf d$ and the board poses that
minimize the reprojection error
\[
E=\sum_{i,j}\bigl\lVert \mathbf u_{ij}-\pi\bigl(K,\mathbf d,R_i,\mathbf t_i,\mathbf X_j\bigr)\bigr\rVert^2 ,
\]
where $\mathbf u_{ij}$ is the detected corner $j$ in view $i$ and $\pi$ is the
projection above.  The result is
$f_x=\calFx$, $f_y=\calFy$, $c_x=\calCx$, $c_y=\calCy$ (pixels),
$\mathbf d=(\calKone,\ \calKtwo,\ \calPone,\ \calPtwo,\ \calKthree)$, and an RMS
reprojection error of \calErr\ px.  Each frame is then corrected with
\texttt{cv2.undistort(frame, K, d)}.

\section{Homography and real-world position}

The floor is a plane, even though its image is tilted by the camera.  The
camera height, tilt and rotation do not need to be measured manually: they are
contained in the relationship between the four detected AprilTag centers and
their known floor coordinates.  After lens distortion is removed, the detected
tag centers give image points $\mathbf u_i=(u_i,v_i)$ and the measured layout
gives floor points $\mathbf X_i=(x_i,y_i)$.  A plane-to-plane projective map is
written in homogeneous coordinates as
\[
\lambda\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{bmatrix}
= H\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad H=(h_{ij})\in\mathbb R^{3\times 3},\quad \lambda\neq 0,
\]
which is equivalent to
\[
x=\frac{h_{11}u+h_{12}v+h_{13}}{h_{31}u+h_{32}v+h_{33}},\qquad
y=\frac{h_{21}u+h_{22}v+h_{23}}{h_{31}u+h_{32}v+h_{33}} .
\]
Multiplying $H$ by a nonzero constant does not change the mapping, so $H$ has
eight degrees of freedom (OpenCV fixes $h_{33}=1$).  Each correspondence
$\mathbf u_i\leftrightarrow\mathbf X_i$ gives two equations that are linear in
the entries of $H$,
\[
h_{11}u_i+h_{12}v_i+h_{13}-x_i\,(h_{31}u_i+h_{32}v_i+h_{33})=0,\qquad
h_{21}u_i+h_{22}v_i+h_{23}-y_i\,(h_{31}u_i+h_{32}v_i+h_{33})=0 ,
\]
so four correspondences, no three of them collinear, determine $H$.  Here
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{detected pixel center} & \text{known floor coordinate}\\
\hline
\text{ID 0 center} & (0,0)\\
\text{ID 1 center} & (W,0)\\
\text{ID 2 center} & (W,H)\\
\text{ID 3 center} & (0,H)
\end{array}
\]
and $W,H$ are the measured center-to-center distances.  The tags provide both
the shape of the coordinate frame and its physical scale.  The division by
$h_{31}u+h_{32}v+h_{33}$ makes the local scale vary over the image, so the
far side of the field, which occupies fewer pixels per millimeter, is handled
automatically; no manual ``millimeters per pixel'' value or camera tilt angle
is needed.  In OpenCV:

\begin{verbatim}
H, _ = cv2.findHomography(image_points, world_points)
floor_point = cv2.perspectiveTransform(pixel_point, H)
\end{verbatim}

After $H$ is saved the tags are not needed in every frame.  The reported
position is the image of the \emph{center of the detection box},
$\mathbf c=\bigl(\tfrac{u_{\min}+u_{\max}}2,\tfrac{v_{\min}+v_{\max}}2\bigr)$,
under $H$.  Strictly, $H$ is exact only for points on the floor plane; the
center of the box lies slightly above the floor because the car has height,
which can add a small systematic offset (Section~\ref{sec:mapping}).  The
camera and tags must remain fixed while the saved $H$ is used.  If the camera
is moved, the lens calibration can usually be reused, but the AprilTag
correspondences and $H$ must be recomputed.

\section{Orientation and velocities}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth]{detection_result_with_matrix.png}
\caption{Example output of the complete localization pipeline.  The
overlay shows the detected car, pixel coordinates, world coordinates in
millimeters (printed as ``world (y, x)'' but in the order $(x,y)$, see
Section~\ref{sec:intro}), linear velocity, orientation $\theta$, angular velocity and
processing FPS.}
\label{fig:detection-output}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Pixel position.}  The displayed image position is the horizontal
center and the bottom edge of the box,
$(u,v)=\bigl(\tfrac{u_{\min}+u_{\max}}2,\ v_{\max}\bigr)$.  The world position
$(x,y)$ is computed from the box center as described in the previous section.

\textbf{Linear velocity.}  For two consecutive detections at times $t_k$ and
$t_{k-1}$,
\[
v_x=\frac{x_k-x_{k-1}}{t_k-t_{k-1}},\qquad
v_y=\frac{y_k-y_{k-1}}{t_k-t_{k-1}} .
\]
This is the motion of the car on the field, not in pixels.  It is a raw
finite difference, without smoothing, and $t_k$ is the time at which the
frame is processed.  After a lost detection the previous sample is discarded,
so the velocity is reported as missing until two consecutive detections exist.

\textbf{Orientation by PCA.}  Let $\mathbf p_1,\dots,\mathbf p_N$ be the pixel
coordinates of the mask, $\bar{\mathbf p}$ their mean, and
\[
\Sigma=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf p_i-\bar{\mathbf p})(\mathbf p_i-\bar{\mathbf p})^{\!\top}.
\]
The principal axis $\mathbf e$ is the unit eigenvector of $\Sigma$ with the
largest eigenvalue, i.e.\ the longest direction of the car.  To obtain the
direction on the floor, the two image points $\bar{\mathbf p}$ and
$\bar{\mathbf p}+100\,\mathbf e$ are both mapped through $H$ to
$(x_0,y_0)$ and $(x_1,y_1)$, which accounts for perspective, and
\[
\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(y_1-y_0,\ x_1-x_0)\cdot\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}.
\]

\textbf{Angular velocity.}
\[
\omega=\frac{\operatorname{wrap}(\theta_k-\theta_{k-1})}{t_k-t_{k-1}},\qquad
\operatorname{wrap}(\delta)=\bigl((\delta+180^\circ)\bmod 360^\circ\bigr)-180^\circ,
\]
so that a step across $+180^\circ/-180^\circ$ is treated as a small rotation.

\subsection{A flaw of the PCA orientation: the sign ambiguity}
\label{sec:pca}

An eigenvector is defined only up to sign: if $\mathbf e$ is a unit
eigenvector of $\Sigma$, so is $-\mathbf e$, and the numerical routine may
return either.  The two choices give angles that differ by $180^\circ$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pca}).  PCA therefore measures the orientation of an
\emph{axis} (a line, $\theta \bmod 180^\circ$), not the heading of the car:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item The front and the back of the car cannot be distinguished.
\item The returned sign can change between consecutive frames, so $\theta$
 can jump by $180^\circ$, for example between $+90^\circ$ and $-90^\circ$
 when the car is aligned with the $y$-axis.  The wrapping function maps such
 a jump to $\pm180^\circ$, so $\omega=\pm180^\circ/(t_k-t_{k-1})$.  At the
 observed $\approx14$\,FPS this is a spurious spike of about
 $\pm2.5\times10^{3}\ {}^\circ/\mathrm{s}$ although the car did not rotate.
\item Orientation errors must be compared modulo $180^\circ$.  The
 evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:eval} does this, so it measures the error of
 the axis and not of a heading.
\end{itemize}

\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth]
\draw[thick, rotate=35] (0,0) ellipse (1.3cm and 0.45cm);
\draw[->, thick, red] (0,0) -- (35:1.9) node[right] {$\mathbf e$: $\theta$};
\draw[->, thick, blue] (0,0) -- (215:1.9) node[left] {$-\mathbf e$: $\theta-180^\circ$};
\draw[gray, dashed] (-2.2,0) -- (2.2,0);
\end{tikzpicture}
\captionof{figure}{Both $\mathbf e$ and $-\mathbf e$ describe the same mask axis;
which one PCA returns is arbitrary, so $\theta$ is known only modulo

\end{center}

A possible remedy, not implemented here, is to keep, among $\theta$ and
$\theta\pm180^\circ$, the value closest to the previous estimate, and to
choose the initial sign from a visual cue such as the windshield, which would
also give a true heading.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

Two aspects are evaluated: whether the car is \emph{detected}, and whether the
detected car is \emph{placed correctly} on the field.  Linear and angular
velocity were not tested against a ground truth.

\subsection{Detection: ROC analysis}

Each camera frame is labelled by the operator as ``car present'' ($1$) or
``car absent'' ($0$) while SAM3 runs on it.  The confidence threshold of SAM3
is set to $0.001$ so that a score is available for every frame; the score of a
frame is the highest instance confidence, or $0$ if nothing is found.  A
detection is predicted whenever the score is at least a threshold $\tau$, and
\[
\mathrm{TPR}(\tau)=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)=\frac{FP}{FP+TN},
\]
where $TP$ are present frames with a detection, $FN$ present frames without,
$FP$ absent frames with a detection and $TN$ absent frames without.  The ROC
curve plots $\mathrm{TPR}$ against $\mathrm{FPR}$ for all $\tau$, its area is
the AUC, and the Youden index $J=\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}$ selects a
compromise threshold.  The recording has 1159 labelled frames, 641 with the
car and 518 without, taken on the raw full frame at input size 448 and
without crop tracking.  The result is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}.

The AUC of $0.891$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}) shows that the score separates the two classes well but
not perfectly.  The threshold used in operation, $\tau=0.3$, is a
high-precision setting: almost no false alarms (FPR $=0.004$) at the price of
missing about 36\,\% of the present frames in this detector-only test.  The
Youden-optimal $\tau=0.216$ gives TPR $=0.736$ at FPR $=0.062$.  This test
does not include the crop tracking of Section~\ref{sec:detect}, which gives the car more
pixels; in the mapping test below the same detector, with tracking, reached a
score of $0.94$--$0.97$ and found the car in every frame, but the effect of
tracking on the ROC was not measured separately.

\subsection{Mapping: position and orientation error}
\label{sec:mapping}

The car is placed at a known field position and orientation $(x^*,y^*,\theta^*)$,
marked with a note as in Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}, and left stationary while 200
consecutive frames are recorded.  Several test cases at different positions
and orientations were evaluated, recorded in separate sessions; the
coordinates of each case refer to the field frame of its own calibration.
With the estimate $(\hat x,\hat y,\hat\theta)$ of a frame, the errors are
\[
e_{\mathrm{pos}}=\sqrt{(\hat x-x^*)^2+(\hat y-y^*)^2},\qquad
e_{\theta}=\Bigl|\bigl((\hat\theta-\theta^*+90^\circ)\bmod180^\circ\bigr)-90^\circ\Bigr| .
\]
The orientation error is taken modulo $180^\circ$ because of the PCA
ambiguity of Section~\ref{sec:pca}; for example $\theta^*=-90^\circ$ and
$+90^\circ$ describe the same axis.

\textbf{Accuracy and precision.}  The frame-to-frame scatter is very small
(Fig.~\ref{fig:offsets} and Table~\ref{tab:bias}): the standard deviation of the
position is at most $0.38$\,mm and that of the angle at most $0.18^\circ$.  The
error is therefore almost entirely a constant offset (bias), not noise: the
estimate is precise, and slightly shifted.  Possible sources, which were not
separated experimentally, are the hand-measured tag spacing, residual lens
distortion, the height of the box center above the floor, and the uncertainty
of placing the car by hand, which is itself of the order of a millimeter and
is not quantified.  The measured errors are close to that limit, so they should
be read as an upper bound on the true system error rather than a precise
value.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{Velocity and angular velocity are not validated.}  They are
 computed as described but were not compared with a ground truth.  They are
 raw, unsmoothed differences of noisy measurements taken at about 14\,FPS, so
 they are noisy.  In the three stationary screenshots of
 Fig.~\ref{fig:cases} the printed speed is below $4$\,mm/s; this is an
 observation, not a test.
\item \textbf{The orientation is an axis, not a heading,} and it can flip by
 $180^\circ$ (Section~\ref{sec:pca}).  This also produces spikes in $\omega$.
\item \textbf{The mapping test is static.}  It covers a few positions with
 200 correlated frames each, and the ground truth was placed by hand.  It does
 not describe accuracy for a moving car, where motion blur and the delay
 between exposure and processing add error.
\item \textbf{The ROC comes from a single recording} with three label blocks
 (absent, present, absent), so its frames are strongly correlated, and it
 measures the detector without crop tracking.
\item \textbf{Scene assumptions.}  The mapping is valid for points on the floor
 plane with the camera and tags fixed; moving the camera requires a new
 homography.  One car is tracked at a time (the highest-scoring instance).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

A calibrated single camera, SAM3 with a text prompt, crop tracking and a
four-tag homography are sufficient to report the position and orientation of
a small RC car on the field.  In the stationary tests the position error was
at most $3.4$\,mm (mean $1.5$\,mm) and the axis orientation error at most
$2.8^\circ$ (mean $1.1^\circ$), the detector reached an AUC of $0.891$, and the
whole pipeline runs at about 14\,FPS.  The main weaknesses are the unvalidated
velocities and the $180^\circ$ ambiguity of the PCA orientation.  A recording
of the complete system is stored in
\texttt{result/final\_result\_car\_detected\_with\_matrix.mp4}
($1280\times720$, 30\,FPS, 178\,s).
